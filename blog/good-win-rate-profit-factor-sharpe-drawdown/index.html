What Is a Good Win Rate, Profit Factor and Sharpe Ratio? And How Much Drawdown Is Acceptable?

Quick answer: a good profit factor is 1.3 to 2.5 after costs, a good Sharpe ratio is above 1, and an acceptable maximum drawdown is roughly under 20 to 25% for stock strategies and under 30 to 40% for crypto strategies. There is no single good win rate: a trend strategy can make money winning only 35% of its trades, if its winners are much bigger than its losers. Anything that looks too good (profit factor above 3, Sharpe above 3, almost no drawdown) usually means too few trades or overfitting.

Key Takeaways

  • Win rate means nothing alone. Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + average win ÷ average loss).
  • Profit factor: 1.3+ is decent, 1.75+ is strong, above 3 is usually too good to be true.
  • Sharpe ratio: 1+ is good, 2+ is very good; always compare with buy and hold.
  • Drawdown: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover. Pick the drawdown you could really sit through, and expect live drawdowns to be deeper.
  • Stocks vs crypto: the same win rate and profit factor rules apply, but crypto strategies usually carry deeper drawdowns.
  • Low win rate strategies can be very profitable if winners are much bigger than losers.

What Is a Good Win Rate?

Short answer: it depends on how big your wins are compared with your losses. A 35% win rate is excellent if winners are three times the size of losers; a 70% win rate loses money if each loss is three times a win. Trend-following strategies typically win 30 to 45% of trades, swing strategies 45 to 60%, and mean-reversion strategies 60 to 75%. The same holds whether you trade Apple or Ethereum.

The Break-Even Win Rate

Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R)

Here R is your average win divided by your average loss, before costs.

R (win ÷ loss)Break-even win rateTypical style
0.567%Mean reversion, scalping (wins half the size of losses)
150%Balanced swing trading
1.540%Swing trading with targets
233%Breakouts
325%Trend following

Costs raise these break-even figures a little, more for strategies that trade often. Read why a backtest wins but loses live for how fees, slippage and funding eat into small edges.

Can a Strategy With a Low Win Rate Still Be Profitable?

Short answer: yes. If your average winner is much larger than your average loser, you can lose most of your trades and still make money. Most trend-following systems, which cut losers quickly and let winners run, win fewer than half their trades.

Worked example: 100 trades, 35% win rate, average win +6%, average loss −2% (R = 3).

  • Wins: 35 × 6% = +210%
  • Losses: 65 × 2% = −130%
  • Net: +80% (simple sum, before compounding and costs). Profit factor = 210 ÷ 130 = 1.6.

The real examples above show the same thing: 46% wins on Bitcoin and 44% on NVIDIA, with profit factors of 1.67 and 1.62, because winners were larger than losers.

The Catch With Low Win Rates

Low win rates mean long losing streaks. At a 35% win rate, 8 or more losses in a row are likely to happen at some point over a few hundred trades. That is psychologically hard, and it is why many traders abandon good trend systems at the worst moment. The Catch With High Win Rates

Very high win rates (80%+) often come from tight targets and wide or no stops. They win small most of the time and occasionally lose big, for example when a stock gaps down on earnings or a coin crashes overnight. Always check the largest loss and the profit factor, not just the win rate.

What Is a Good Profit Factor?

Short answer: profit factor is gross profit divided by gross loss, after costs. Above 1.0 makes money; 1.3 to 1.75 is decent; 1.75 to 2.5 is strong. Above 3 usually signals too few trades or overfitting rather than a great strategy.

PF = gross profit ÷ gross loss

Profit factor combines win rate and win size into one number, which makes it more useful than win rate alone. A profit factor of 1.5 means you earned $1.50 for every $1.00 lost. Because live trading is usually worse than a backtest, a strategy with a backtest profit factor of 1.1 has little room for error, especially on stocks with high commissions or small coins with wide spreads.

What Is a Good Sharpe Ratio?

Short answer: an annualized Sharpe ratio above 1 is good, above 2 is very good, and below 0.5 is weak. Above 3 in a backtest deserves suspicion. Always compare with the Sharpe ratio of buy and hold for the same asset and period.

Sharpe = excess return ÷ volatility

"Excess return" is the average return minus the risk-free rate, and "volatility" is the standard deviation of returns, both annualized. A higher Sharpe means a smoother ride for the same return.

  • Stocks: broad stock indexes have historically had long-run Sharpe ratios of roughly 0.3 to 0.6, so a stock strategy above 1 is notable. Returns are annualized with about 252 trading days a year.
  • Crypto: returns and volatility are both high; in strong bull years Bitcoin's own Sharpe has exceeded 1, so beating buy and hold matters more than the raw number. Crypto trades all 365 days, so returns are annualized over 365 days.
  • Related ratios: the Sortino ratio counts only downside volatility; the Calmar ratio divides annual return by maximum drawdown.

NVIDIA shows why the comparison matters: a Sharpe of 0.92 looks respectable, but the stock itself returned +2,954% over the same period, so the strategy added work and risk without adding return.

How Much Drawdown Is Acceptable?

Short answer: the drawdown you could live through with real money without abandoning the strategy. As rough guides, many traders aim for a maximum drawdown under 20 to 25% for stock strategies and under 30 to 40% for crypto strategies, and well below the asset's own buy-and-hold drawdown. Expect live drawdowns up to 1.5 to 2 times the backtest's worst.

Drawdown Depth and Duration

Check both how deep and how long. The Bitcoin strategy had a −54% maximum drawdown and stayed below its previous peak for 861 days. That is shallower than holding Bitcoin over the same period (which fell about 77% at worst), but over two years under water is more than most people can sit through. The NVIDIA version's drawdown was smaller at −39%, but it took even longer, 888 days, to recover.

How to Reduce Drawdown

  • Smaller position size, or risk-based sizing: BitLogic Backtester can size each trade so that a stop-out loses a fixed % of the account.
  • A trend filter, such as trading only above the 200-day moving average.
  • Stops and trailing stops, remembering that stocks can gap past a stop overnight (see our stop-loss and take-profit guide).
  • Diversify across several stocks or coins with Test on many.
  • Lower or no leverage, especially on crypto futures.

For a realistic range rather than one historical path, use the Monte Carlo tab: it reshuffles your trades thousands of times and shows how bad drawdowns could plausibly get.

Read Metrics Together, Never Alone

  1. Did it beat buy and hold? If not, the strategy isn't worth the effort. (Bitcoin: yes. NVIDIA: no.)
  2. Is the sample big enough? 100+ trades, and a luck check that passes. See how many trades you need.
  3. Profit factor and Sharpe in the good range, after costs.
  4. Drawdown you can live with, in depth and duration.
  5. Holds up out of sample, in walk-forward testing, and in paper trading.

FAQ

What is a good win rate for a trading strategy?

There is no single good win rate; it depends on the reward-to-risk ratio. Trend-following strategies are often profitable at 30 to 45% wins, swing strategies at 45 to 60%, and mean-reversion strategies often win 60 to 75% with smaller wins. The break-even win rate is 1 divided by (1 + average win / average loss), before costs. This is the same for stocks and crypto.

What is a good profit factor?

Profit factor is gross profit divided by gross loss, after costs. Below 1.0 loses money, 1.0 to 1.3 is marginal, 1.3 to 1.75 is decent, 1.75 to 2.5 is strong, and above 3 usually means too few trades or overfitting.

What is a good Sharpe ratio?

An annualized Sharpe ratio below 0.5 is weak, 0.5 to 1 is acceptable, 1 to 2 is good and above 2 is very good. Backtest Sharpe ratios above 3 should be treated with suspicion. Compare against buy and hold of the same stock or coin over the same period.

How much drawdown is acceptable?

The drawdown you could live through without abandoning the strategy. Many traders aim for a maximum drawdown under about 20 to 25% for stock strategies and under about 30 to 40% for crypto strategies, and at least well below the asset's own buy-and-hold drawdown. Expect live drawdowns up to 1.5 to 2 times the backtest's.

Can a strategy with a low win rate be profitable?

Yes. With an average win three times the average loss, a strategy breaks even at 25% wins before costs, and a 35 to 40% win rate is very profitable. Many trend-following systems on stocks and crypto win fewer than half their trades.

Is a 50% win rate good?

It is good if your average win is larger than your average loss after costs. At equal win and loss sizes, 50% only breaks even before fees.

Is a profit factor of 2 good?

Yes, a profit factor of 2 after costs, with 100+ trades, is strong. Check that it holds on other markets and in walk-forward testing.

Is a Sharpe ratio of 1 good?

Yes. An annualized Sharpe ratio of 1 is generally considered good, provided it beats buy and hold of the same asset.

Is a 30% drawdown too much?

For most stock strategies it is on the high side; for crypto it is moderate. What matters is whether you would keep following the strategy through it.

What matters more, win rate or profit factor?

Profit factor, because it includes both how often you win and how much. Win rate tells you mainly how many losing streaks to expect.

Are good backtest metrics different for stocks and crypto?

Win rate and profit factor benchmarks are the same. Drawdown and Sharpe differ: crypto is far more volatile, so a crypto strategy can accept a deeper drawdown than a stock strategy, and Bitcoin itself has fallen more than 75% from its peak. Annualize stock returns with about 252 trading days and crypto with 365, and always compare with buy and hold of the same asset.

The Bottom Line

Good numbers look the same on a stock or a coin: a profit factor of 1.3 or more after costs, a Sharpe ratio around 1 or better, a drawdown you could really sit through, and enough trades to trust. But no single metric decides it. Read them together, compare them with buy and hold, and check them on data the strategy has never seen.
