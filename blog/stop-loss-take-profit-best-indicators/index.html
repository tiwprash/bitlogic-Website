How Should I Set a Stop-Loss and Take-Profit?

Short answer: set the stop-loss at the price that proves your trade idea wrong (beyond a recent swing level, or a multiple of ATR from the entry), size the position from that stop, and set the take-profit as a multiple of the stop distance (1.5R to 3R), or use a trailing stop instead of a fixed target for trend-following strategies. Then backtest several versions and keep the one that works across a range of values and markets.

Why ATR Stops Work on Both Stocks and Crypto

The Average True Range (ATR) measures how far a market normally moves per candle. A stop of 2 × ATR is the same rule everywhere, but it gives very different distances. Typical daily figures (they change with market conditions):

MarketTypical daily ATR2 × ATR stopA fixed 3% stop would be
Large-cap stockAbout 1 to 2%About 2 to 4%About right, or a little wide
BitcoinAbout 3 to 5%About 6 to 10%Inside normal daily noise
Small altcoinOften 6 to 10%+12 to 20%+Hit almost at random

That is why a strategy tested with one fixed percentage on Apple and on Solana usually fails on one of them. With an ATR stop and risk-based sizing, the same rules carry the same risk on both.

Step-by-Step: Setting a Stop-Loss

  1. Decide where the idea is wrong. For a breakout above a 20-day high, the idea fails if the price falls back well inside the range, for example below the 10-day low or 2 ATR below the entry.
  2. Prefer volatility-based distances. Bitcoin can move 3 to 5% on a normal day; a large-cap stock 1 to 2%. A 2% stop is noise on Bitcoin and wide on a utility stock. ATR scales automatically.
  3. Size from the stop. Risk-based sizing makes each stop-out cost the same share of the account (for example 1%), whatever the stop distance. BitLogic Backtester offers this as "risk % of the account".
  4. Remember stops can gap. Stocks gap overnight and on earnings; crypto can wick through levels in seconds. A realistic backtest fills a gapped stop at the open, not at your stop price. BitLogic Backtester does this.
  5. Test a range. Try 1.5, 2, 2.5 and 3 ATR. If only one value works, it is probably luck.

Position Size From the Stop: a Worked Example

Size = risk amount ÷ stop distance

With a $10,000 account and 1% risk per trade, you risk $100. On a stock with a 4% stop, the position is $100 ÷ 4% = $2,500. On Bitcoin with an 8% stop, it is $100 ÷ 8% = $1,250. Both trades lose the same $100 if stopped out, before costs and gaps.

Step-by-Step: Setting a Take-Profit

  1. Match the target to the strategy type. Mean reversion (buy oversold, sell on the bounce) works with close targets; trend following needs room for big winners.
  2. For swing trades, start at 2R and compare 1.5R and 3R.
  3. For trend following, try no target and a trailing stop, or an exit signal such as a close below a moving average. Capping winners can ruin a trend system's profit factor.
  4. Check the win rate it implies. At 2R you need about 33% wins to break even before costs; at 3R, 25%. See what is a good win rate and profit factor.

Trailing Stops

A trailing stop follows the price at a set distance (a % or ATR multiple) below the best price since entry, and never moves back. It locks in profit while letting winners run. Too tight, and normal pullbacks stop you out; too loose, and you give back most of the gain. In a correct backtest the trail only moves on previous candles; moving it on the current candle's high and then checking that candle's low assumes an order of events the data can't show (see look-ahead bias).

Example: a Breakout Strategy With an ATR Stop and a Trailing Exit

  • Entry: close above the 20-day Donchian upper band.
  • Stop-loss: 2 × ATR(14) below entry.
  • Exit: close below the 10-day Donchian lower band, or a 10% trailing stop.
  • Size: risk 1% of the account per trade.

Which Indicators or Combinations Work Best?

Short answer: no indicator works best in every market. The combinations that hold up best combine three jobs: a trend filter (such as the 200-period moving average, Supertrend or ADX), an entry trigger (such as RSI, Stochastic, MACD or a Donchian or Bollinger breakout) and a risk measure (ATR for stops and sizing). Use indicators from different families, keep it to two or three conditions, and confirm with backtests on many markets.

FAQ

How should I set a stop-loss?

Place the stop where your trade idea is proven wrong, not at a random percentage: beyond a recent swing low or high, or a multiple of ATR (often 1.5 to 3 ATR) from the entry. Then size the position so that hitting the stop loses a fixed small share of the account, commonly 0.5 to 2%. Backtest several stop distances and prefer a range that works, not one exact value.

How should I set a take-profit?

Use a risk-reward multiple of the stop distance (for example 1.5R to 3R) for swing and mean-reversion strategies, or no fixed target with a trailing stop for trend-following strategies, which depend on a few large winners. Test both in a backtest.

What percentage should my stop-loss be?

There is no universal percentage. A 3% stop is wide for a calm large-cap stock and normal noise for Bitcoin. Base the stop on volatility (ATR) or a swing level, then size the position so a stop-out loses about 0.5 to 2% of your account.

What is an ATR stop-loss?

An ATR stop-loss is placed a multiple of the Average True Range away from the entry, for example 2 × ATR(14). Because ATR measures how much a market normally moves, the stop is automatically wider on volatile coins and tighter on calm stocks.

What is a good risk-reward ratio?

Commonly 1:1.5 to 1:3. A higher ratio needs a lower win rate to break even: 1:2 breaks even at about 33% wins before costs, 1:3 at 25%.

Should I always use a take-profit?

No. Trend-following strategies often do better with a trailing stop or an exit signal and no fixed target. Backtest both versions.

Which indicators work best?

No indicator works best everywhere. Robust combinations pair one indicator for trend direction (such as a 200-period moving average or Supertrend), one for entry timing (such as RSI, Stochastic or a breakout), and one for risk (ATR). Use indicators from different families rather than several that measure the same thing, and validate on many markets.

What is the best indicator for crypto?

There is no single best one. Trend tools (moving averages, Supertrend, Donchian breakouts) with ATR stops are widely used for crypto because of its strong trends. Test them on several coins.

What is the best indicator for stocks?

Common robust choices are the 200-day moving average as a trend filter with RSI or Bollinger Bands for pullback entries. Validate on several stocks and markets.

How many indicators should I use?

Usually two or three, from different families. More conditions mean fewer trades and a higher risk of overfitting.

The Bottom Line

Put the stop where the idea fails, measure it in ATR rather than a fixed percentage, and size the trade from the stop so every loss costs the same. Use a 1.5R to 3R target for swing trades and a trailing stop for trends. Pick two or three indicators from different families, and backtest every choice on many stocks or coins before trusting it.
